Advantages and Disadvantages of Green Roofs

From an aerial perspective, the UK’s towns and cities aren’t appealing, with a sea of black and grey as far as the eye can see. This doesn’t have to be the case though, and in recent years a growing movement has made its way to British shores.

Looking great, energy efficient, and environmentally friendly, green roofs better utilise an often neglected area. As with anything though, there are considerations to weigh. Below we provide an honest, balanced appraisal of both the advantages and drawbacks of implementing a living roof.

The Advantages

1. Improved Drainage & Flood Prevention

As a result of dense urban development, up to 75% of rainwater runs off paved surfaces immediately into sewers. Green roofs store water in substrate and plants, naturally returning it to the atmosphere via evapotranspiration and attenuating runoff by up to 80%.

2. Triple the Lifespan of the Roof

Roofs suffer from extreme UV exposure, hail, and fluctuating freeze-thaw cycles. The botanical barrier doubles or triples the life expectancy of the waterproofing membrane, protecting building investments for decades.

3. Enhanced Thermal Performance

Typical roofs suffer substantial heat loss in winter and overheated top floors in summer. Green roofs provide passive cooling in hot months (reducing AC power) and reduce winter heating bills by over 10%.

4. Aiding Air Quality & Pollution Capture

Air pollution causes over 24,000 premature deaths in the UK annually. Studies prove green roofs help reduce up to 37% of sulphur dioxide, 21% of nitrous acid, and bind up to 0.2 kg of dust particles per m² each year.

The Drawbacks & Engineering Considerations

Initial Capital Expense

Green roofs carry a higher initial investment than basic felt or asphalt. However, over time the tripled membrane lifespan, reduced HVAC bills, and elevated property valuation more than offset the upfront outlay.

Structural Weight Load

Typically, living roofs add between 70 and 200 kg/m² to an existing rooftop. Flat reinforced roofs can usually accommodate this load with ease, but structural capacity must always be verified by an engineer.

Routine Maintenance

A green roof is a living system. While extensive sedum requires minimal intervention, regular seasonal weeding, drain clearance, and fertilizer replenishment are required to maintain peak botanical health.

Planning Policy & Regulations

The Architect's Guide to Urban Greening Factor (UGF) & BNG

Under the London Plan Policy G5 and emerging UK local council frameworks, major developments must calculate an Urban Greening Factor (UGF) score.

Extensive green roofs with a minimum substrate depth of 80mm contribute a UGF score of 0.7 per unit area, while intensive green roofs score 0.8. Coupled with mandatory 10% Biodiversity Net Gain (BNG) regulations, living roofs are frequently the most space-efficient and cost-effective mechanism for securing rapid planning approval.
